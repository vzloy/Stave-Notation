1. Two Frameworks, One Container

Geometric algebra (GA) and metric geometry address orthogonal aspects of the same spatial reality. GA gives an algebra of transformations: rotations, reflections, and rigid motions composed by multiplying rotors and motors. Metric geometry gives a calculus of distances: Hausdorff distance between shapes, Wasserstein between distributions, geodesics through obstacles.

In Stave Notation, both enter through the & operator — the container that carries spatial structure alongside data. GA operations are primarily φ (transforms that preserve or scale the metric) and ρ (representation changes between algebraic encodings). Metric geometry operations span all morphism types — μ reads the metric, σ partitions by it, τ rearranges according to it.

The key insight: GA provides the morphisms (how to transform), metric geometry provides the enrichment (how to measure what those transformations did). A GA rotor is an isometry — it preserves the metric. Metric geometry classifies that fact. Neither replaces the other.

Key principle
GA products map to stave cap operations. Inner product a · b is grade ↓ (⊃ cap, contraction/projection). Outer product a ∧ b is grade ↑ (⊂ cap, span/surface from edges). Geometric product ab is mixed grades (□ box, rotation/reflection).

Why this matters
The type row determines what measurements are available. A surface mesh with bivector normals (B ∈ ⋀²G³) gives you Gaussian curvature directly — the wedge product computes the normal, the geometric product measures turning. A contour with ℝ² coordinates must approximate curvature from finite differences. The encoding choice is a choice about which μ boxes downstream can see.

5. The & Bridge — Where GA Meets Metric

The & container carries the metric structure that both frameworks read and write. GA operations primarily transform data while the metric & passes through (isometry) or scales (Lipschitz). Metric geometry operations read the metric to measure, partition, or rearrange.

What happens to metric & under each operation type

GA operations — effect on metric &

φ: Rotor R = e^(Bθ/2)         & unchanged      (isometry: d_out = d_in)
φ: Motor M ∈ G³˙¹              & unchanged      (isometry: rigid motion)
φ: Versor (reflection)         & unchanged      (isometry)
φ: Dilator D = e^(αI/2)       & scaled by e^α  (Lipschitz, L = e^α)
ρ: EmbedCGA                   & form changes    (Euclidean → conformal inner product)
ρ: MarchingCubes               & form changes    (voxel grid → mesh connectivity)

Metric geometry operations — effect on metric &

μ: Distance, Hausdorff, ...    & consumed       (read &, produce scalar)
σ: Voronoi, DBSCAN, ...       & preserved       (partition, & unchanged)
φ: Isometry                    & unchanged       (L = 1 both ways)
φ: LipschitzMap                & scaled ≤ L      (bounded distortion)
ρ: MetricGraph, MDS, ...      & form changes     (new metric representation)
τ: kNN-Graph, ε-Net, ...      & preserved       (rearrange, & unchanged)

The convergence point is isometry. A GA rotor that satisfies RR̃ = 1 is exactly an isometry — it preserves the metric. A metric geometry Isometry with L = 1 both ways is exactly what that rotor computes. The mathematical content is the same; the two frameworks describe it from opposite ends.

The quadratic form IS the metric

In GA, the quadratic form Q(v) = v² that defines a Clifford algebra is a metric. The signature (p,q,r) determines the geometry: Euclidean (n,0,0), Minkowski (3,1,0), conformal (n+1,1,0). In CGA, two points a and b become null vectors, and:

d(a,b)² = −2 a·b

The inner product is the distance function. A rotor that preserves the inner product preserves distances. This is why GA transformations and metric geometry measurements compose naturally through the same & container.

8. Category-Theoretic Bridge

For the formally inclined: the two frameworks meet in the theory of enriched categories.

Lawvere enrichment and Clifford homomorphisms

Metric geometry as enriched category (Lawvere, 1973)

A metric space (X, d) is a category enriched over ([0,∞], ≥, +):
  - Objects: points of X
  - Hom(x,y) = d(x,y) ∈ [0,∞]
  - Composition: d(x,z) ≤ d(x,y) + d(y,z)   (triangle inequality)
  - Identity: d(x,x) = 0

Enriched functor F: (X,d_X) → (Y,d_Y):
  d_Y(Fx, Fy) ≤ d_X(x,y)    non-expansive (Lipschitz L ≤ 1)

Geometric algebra as structured algebra

A Clifford algebra Cl(V,Q) with quadratic form Q:
  - Q(v) = v²  defines the metric on V
  - Clifford homomorphism f: Cl(V,Q) → Cl(W,Q')
    preserving Q is an isometry of the underlying metric spaces

The bridge

  GA rotor R with RR̃ = 1
  ⟺ isometry of (V, Q)
  ⟺ enriched functor with d_Y = d_X (equality, not just ≤)

  GA versor with |VṼ| = k²
  ⟺ Lipschitz map with L = k
  ⟺ enriched functor with d_Y ≤ k · d_X

In Stave: both are φ-morphisms with specific & behaviour.
The category theory makes the & classification theorem a fact:
  Isometry → & unchanged
  Lipschitz → & scaled ≤ L
  Metric-consuming → & read then dropped

The & classification table from the metric geometry reference is not just a convention — it's a theorem in enriched category theory. An isometry (GA rotor) preserves the enrichment exactly. A Lipschitz map (GA dilator) scales the enrichment by at most L. A measurement (μ) is a functor from the enriched category to ℝ that reads the hom-values — it consumes the enrichment. The notation makes this visible: the & column in a stave tells you what kind of enriched functor the morphism is.

Summary

GA and metric geometry are not competing frameworks. GA provides the algebra of transformations — rotors compose, motors interpolate, bivectors compute curvature. Metric geometry provides the calculus of distances — Hausdorff compares shapes, Wasserstein compares distributions, kNN builds graphs, Sholl counts branches.

In Stave Notation, both enter through & — the container that carries metric structure alongside data. GA operations are primarily φ and ρ; metric geometry spans all morphism types. The bridge is the quadratic form: Q(v) = v² defines both the Clifford algebra and the distance function. A rotor that preserves Q is an isometry that preserves d.

For the biologist, the notation hides the formalism: φ: Rotor and μ: Hausdorff compose with ; through the same & container, and the classification table says what happens to metric structure at each step. The two frameworks complement each other the way a screwdriver and a ruler complement each other — one does the work, the other measures the result.
